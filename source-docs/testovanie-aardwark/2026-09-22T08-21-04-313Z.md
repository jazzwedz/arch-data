Implementation of ITSM Foundations with a Light Touch
With a 15-person tech team operating on a Java/React/AWS stack, the absence of ITSM process foundations will inevitably
lead to technical debt manifesting for example as critical alerts during affiliate website night (from slovak time pertspective)
launches in Asian markets.
ITSM is a key to scale reliably - particularly when internationalization drives exponential growth in platform interactions.
Introduction of ITSM basics using tools like JIRA Service Management (JSM), but with an emphasis on process lightness.
Careful here - definitely not the cumbersome ITIL approach, but rather a "startup ITSM" focusing on:
Automated incident management workflow
Self-service catalog for internal non-IT teams
Simple change management processes for production deployments
Reasonably and smartly managed Knowledge base with documentation
Internationalization means that more and more people start using the product, with greater diversity of use cases and time
zones. Without a basic structure for incident and problem management, the team can easily fall into "fire-fighting" mode.
JIRA Service Management (there are many other similar solutions as well) would provide us with:
Transparent metrics about incidents that reveal weak spots in quality
Problem-solving processes that work even at 3 AM (when it's working hours in Singapore)
Scalable knowledge management
As I learned from the interview, you're already using JIRA. JSM is a different product, but it works well with JIRA.
Risks and compromises:
🔸 Team resistance to "processes" - that's why I would start very gently and only with the essential basics
🔸 Risk of being overwhelmed with tickets - need to be smart with reasonable automation
🔸 Compromise between speed and control - but "light" ITSM and a reasonable, human, respectful approach can achieve
both
🔸 JSM costs money, it's licensed similarly to standard JIRA (I assume you're using cloud and not data center)

Attracting Top Talents
The reality of Bratislava is that talents are difficult to find and probablyy even harder to retain.
One of the companies (where I worked) was significantly above average in this regard. Since I was one of those conducting
interviews, I not only observed this but was also deeply interested in why seemingly talented experts wanted to work
specifically there, when the IT professional market is essentially global and Developer Experience (DevEx) is almost similar
everywhere.
I managed to understand that the company had an excellent reputation in the context of its tech stack.
I remember a specific candidate from Košice mentioned that he had heard about this company during a lecture as being
technologically exceptionally progressive.
Only then did I realize the chain reaction. The company managed to attract top specialists with interesting salaries, who
naturally began to shape the technological environment in their own image. The company gave significant space to R&D
activities. They tested the newest technologies even before their acceptance by the broader market. People thus had the
opportunity to try very interesting technologies in practice - those that elsewhere were only talked about or taught in
lectures.
This spread relatively quickly - of course, one shouldn't avoid tech and developer conferences. Through this, the company
gained significant credit and reputation - so talents often preferred it even for a lower salary. (Lower compared to other
companies. 🙂, not low in general.)
Of course, I don't mean to say that the company saves on salaries. These must, of course, be competitive, but with this
approach to technology, it can gain "something extra," a reason why significant talents will be satisfied there, and why it will
be more interesting than competitors in the labor market when choosing an employer.
The mentioned company often implemented technologies that had not yet crossed the so-called “chasm” (in tech lifecycle).
This is the point beyond which a technology becomes generally accepted. See the image below pleas4.
As Head of Technology, I would like to ask for the support of key stakeholders to achieve this effect.
Top talents mean top results.

Creating Two IT Teams
The idea here is dividing the 15-member tech team into two flat teams - "Core Product" and "Growth & Integration" team.
To support the transformation of the architecture into a domain-based modular system (Domain-Driven Design) specifically:
Core Product Team (8 people) - focused on the domain of global API core and business logic
Growth & Integration Team (7 people) - specializing in the presentation layer and localized components
Each team would have strong autonomy over their own microservices. Benefits:
Faster market adaptation. The Growth team can develop localized versions in parallel without interfering with the core
business
Local autonomy with global consistency. Teams can work independently on their domains without waiting for others, while
still adhering to standards
Team spirit, onboarding acceleration, ... New team members can orient themselves more quickly in smaller, clearly defined
domains
Each team would have strong autonomy, but they would share a common DevEx platform including:
Standardized CI/CD pipelines with automated tests
Self-service infrastructure through Infra as Code, ...
Unified monitoring and observability stack
Centralized documentation and shared best practices
Both could start to operate on the Kanban with visually guided workflow in JIRA. However, if classic sprints wouldn’t be too
much for the culture or attitude, the we could user so called SCRUMBAN.
Each team would have its tech lead, but without unnecessary hierarchies.
Why it's important in our growth phase?
You have 15 people who must handle core development, internationalization, and technical debt. One monolithic team
cannot effectively prioritize all these areas simultaneously.
Two teams structure would enable:
Better predictability of deliveries without rigid sprints, more transparency, dependecies, ...
Clear visualization of WIP limits, which would reduce context-switching
Risks and compromises:
Communication overhead, Risk of creating "silos": Two teams need clear boundaries and regular synchronization - I
would address this with regular guild meetings across teams
Increased infrastructure complexity: needed more sophisticated infrastructure
Risk of inconsistent APIs: Shared contract between teams can be challenging - the solution would be implementing
Contract-First development and API governance
Cultural change: Transitioning from monolith to microservices requires a mindset change
Need to rethink resource allocation and probably on-call rotations, etc.
I would stick to flat structure! However, creating Technical Product/Service Lead roles could be motivating for senior
developers (even without “hierarchiacal” promotions)

Which Project Comes First
I won't write anything different than what I mentioned during the personal interview.
Here it's very important to rely on the company's vision, mission, and strategy.
I'll continue working with the assumption that the company is in the growth phase and is interested
in strengthening its existing portfolio of products and services.
Gartner's Run, Grow, Transform (RGT) Model is a classic model of managing IT in an enterprise. This
approach aligns with the way businesses have been traditionally managed and guide IT investments
accordingly.
Similarly, I would use a framework for goal management, trying to understand KPIs or highly
important tasks that may stand alongside these goals. Of course, we need to consider legislative
requirements and potential regulators.
I would also take into account the OPS situation, what state it's in, and what are the intentions of
colleagues from infrastructure, cloud, or other supporting technologies. Specifically, within
problem management, I would search for the causes of the most frequent incidents.
I would also try not to forget about potential technical debt and its refactoring.
In my practice, the WSJF approach has proven most effective. It's based on assessing these
dimensions:
1.Value to the business and/or user
2.Time criticality
3.Risk reduction and/or opportunity enablement
By adding these values, we get the so-called cost of delay, which according to the WSJF model is
divided by Job Duration (or Size)

B. Refactoring pipeline and CI/CD
During growth, resources are primarily directed toward revenue-generating activities rather
than infrastructure improvements.
As long as the current pipeline functions adequately, the urgency to refactor diminishes
compared to market expansion opportunities.
Unlike new market entry, the benefits of refactoring are difficult to quantify in immediate
financial terms.
The process of refactoring itself carries operational risks that companies prefer to avoid
during rapid growth periods.
Yes, An unstable pipeline is like a ticking bomb - you never know when it will explode. If
deployments are failing and the feedback loop is slow, everything else slows down.
Most critical from a risk perspective. We will focus on this in the next quarter.
C. Automated integration tests
Reducing testing time by 50% sounds amazing, but... several practical reasons:
During growth, we prioritize customer-facing features over internal process improvements
like testing automation.
Developing automated tests requires dedicated engineering time that may be redirected to
new market opportunities.
Making existing code testable often necessitates significant refactoring, creating additional
scope beyond the testing itself.

Mentoring Case
# One of your senior Java developers has reservations about the architectural direction (e.g., moving toward
modularization and microservices). He considers it unnecessary complication and claims the team has other priorities.
How would you communicate with this developer?
Describe:
What stance would you take (both technically and managerially),
How would you explain your perspective and decision,
How would you ensure he doesn't lose motivation while moving the discussion forward.
The stance I would take (both technically and managerially)
I would take a pragmatic, not dogmatic stance. Meaning: I wouldn't see the situation in black and white as "modular
architecture vs. monolith," but rather as an opportunity for dialogue about real problems.
Personally, I’m definitely not convinced that microservices are a cure-all. They bring many new challenges. Have you ever
heard “Microservices Hell”?
Managerially, I would show respect for the opinions of an experienced Java developer - his concerns can't just be
"resistance to change," but may stem from deep technical knowledge.
How I would explain my perspective and decision?
I would explain the decision not just in the context of technical architecture, but also business needs and company’s
growth phase (RGT model above).
I might say something like:
"I understand your concerns and value your perspective.
You're right that modularization adds complexity, and I also agree we have many priorities. But let me explain why this step
is important from a long-term perspective. Last evening, I was "hit between the horns" by insights from our analytics data -
growth in international regions is increasing 30% faster than in our domestic market. This means our monolithic
architecture will soon hit limitations when implementing new partners.
So, it's about the ability to deliver value faster. Modularization will allow us to parallelize work, accelerate deployment, and
give the team greater autonomy. It's not about following hype and buzz, but about solving real problems that we're already
beginning to feel."
How I would ensure the developer doesn't lose motivation
I would definitely start with respect developer’s expertise: "I take your concerns seriously. Can you help me identify the
risks of modularization so we can actively address them?"
If possible, I would involve the developer in the decision-making process: "I'd like you to be part of the team that defines
boundaries between modules. Your experience with our monolith is invaluable."
Offer space for personal growth and experimentation: "Let's start with a pilot project - select one domain to separate.
You'll be the technical lead for this experiment, and after a month, we'll evaluate together whether it's bringing the
expected benefits. What would you say to TOGAF certification, to have even better overview on the Architecture?"
I would explore deeper personal motivation: "What do you enjoy most about your work? If it's deep solving of complex
problems, microservices architecture can give you interesting new challenges."
